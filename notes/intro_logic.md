## What is Logic?


**Logic** is a systematic study of the rules and principles used to distinguish correct reasoning from incorrect reasoning. It is a science of drawing a conclusion given a set of assumptions. It allows to validate whether an argument is valid. 

Logic is an ancient subject dated back in the history, e.g. Trivium from the ancience greece (5th - 4th century BC), Mohist from the warring states of ancient China (4th - 3th centry BC), Hetuvidyā from the ancient India (4th centry CE).

Logic forms an important mathematic core of the modern Computer Science. 

## Why Logic is useful?

Logic is widely used, in areas of 

* Math proof
* Medical diagnosis
* Debugging computer programs 
    * Catching AI hallucination
* Legal reasonings
* Playing games
* Solving puzzles

## Elements of Logics

The logic systems are built up from the following elements from smallest to the largest

* Logical sentences (Logical statements)
* Logical entailment
* Logical proofs

### Logical Statement

A logical statement is the smallest unit a logical system in the general sense. 
A logical statement can be used to describe a possible set of worlds (or instances of the domain).

### Logical Entailment

Logical entailment is a way to describe how two logical sentences are related to each other.

For example, consider another statement

`(S3) Everyone follows at least one person.`

We would expect that are many worlds that satisfy `(S3)`.

An observation here is that every world that satisfies `(S2)` also satisfies `(S3)`. 

We say *`(S2)` logically entails `(S3)`*.

#### Questions

1. Does `(S1)` entail `(S3)`?
1. Does `(S3)` entail `(S2)`?


### Logical Proof 

A logical proof is a process 

1. to draw a conclusion (a logical statement) 
1. from a set of premises (a group of logical statements that are connected via conjunction and disjunction)
1. via a sequence of inference steps.


#### Logical Inference

There are at least 2 kinds of logical inference operations.

1. Deduction
1. Induction 

##### Deductive reasoning

In deductive reasoning, we move from general statements to specific statements, e.g. 

`(S4) adding one to every even number produces an odd number`.

We can argue

`(S5) 1 + 2 is an odd number`.

Another example 

`(S6) all Term 7 CSD students must have taken Elements of Construction.`

and

`(S7) all students who take Elements of Construction must have taken Information System and Programming.` 

Therefore

`(S8) all Term 7 CSD students must have taken Information System and Programming.`


##### Inductive reasoning

In inductive reasoning, we infer general statements from specific statements. 

For instance

`(S9) I've seen 100 black mynas in Bedok.`

Therefore I think

`(S10) all mynas are black.`

This seems to be a valid argument, especially for science and discovery. However it is not 
very suitable for verification. 

Because `(S10)` might not be valid because I might have missed a leucistic myna.

Hence for Logical proof, we largely avoid induction, except for a special instance, namely structural induction, which will be discussed shortly.

##### Logical Proof vs Model checking

An alternative to logical proof is to enumerate all instances that satisfy the premises and check whether all of them satisfy the conclusion. This is process is called model checking. 

One advantage of model checking over Logical proof is that it is automatable. 
One obvious disadvantage of model checking is that it is limited to finite set of instances.

## Why Natural Languages fail here

Natural languages are *ambiguous* by nature. A grammatically correct sentence is ambiguous if there are at least two different interpretation (meaning) of the same syntactic form. 

* There’s a girl in the room with a telescope. 
    - a girl is in the room in in which the telescope is installed; or a girl holding a telescope in a room?
* Crowds Rushing to See Pope Trample 6 to Death.
    - crowds trample 6 or the Pope trample 6?
* Scientists Grow Frog Eyes and Ears
    - Frog eyes and for ears are on the scientists faces; or they are created in the lab by the scientists? 
* Wife tells husband, "go to the supermarket to buy a water melon; if you see eggs, buy ten." 
    - Should the husband come back from the supermarket with one water melon or ten water melons?

To avoid ambiguity, matheticicans and computer scientists use mathematical symbols and operators to form logical sentences. We call it symbolic logic or formal logic.
